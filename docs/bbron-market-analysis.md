## 1. The product at the centre

B. Braun's offering in the robotic-knee-arthroplasty conversation is the **Aesculap OrthoPilot® Elite** — an image-free navigation system (handheld "Multitool" interface) that guides the positioning of cutting jigs rather than driving a robotic arm. In other words, it is the **navigation-first archetype that every current "robot" on the market evolved from**.

Documented OrthoPilot Elite characteristics we can lean on:

- **Image-free.** No pre-op CT or MRI required — no additional radiation, no planning lead-time, no imaging cost passed through to the payer.
- **>100 international studies** on clinical effectiveness of the OrthoPilot family.
- **28% reduction** in intra-operative data-acquisition time in TKA 6 vs. the preceding software generation.
- **Auto-documentation** of resections, implant sizes, pre/post-op leg axes and procedure duration — removes admin burden from the surgical team.
- **Ergonomic handheld Multitool** — "four instruments in one" — designed for low footprint and list mobility.

## 2. Competitive context

| System | Capex (approx.) | Imaging | Mechanism | Patient-pull marketing | Lock-in |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Stryker Mako** | ~£1M / ~$1M | Pre-op CT required | Robotic arm + bone saw | National DTC ("Scan. Plan. Mako Can.", Aug 2023) — linear TV, streaming, social, search | Triathlon / Mako-only implant family |
| **Zimmer Biomet ROSA** | Below Mako (not disclosed) | X-ray or image-free | Robotic cutting guide | Co-marketing assets for hospitals, less national DTC heft | Persona / Vanguard implants |
| **J&J DePuy Velys** | Mid-market | Image-free | Robotic arm, compact | Surgeon-led marketing | Attune implants |
| **Smith+Nephew CORI** | Mid-market | Image-free | Handheld robotic burr | Some co-marketing | Journey II / Legion |
| **B. Braun OrthoPilot Elite** | Fraction of above (order-of-magnitude lower) | Image-free | Navigation-guided jig | **None at scale** | Columbus / VEGA System 7000 implants |

Two structural observations:

1. **The "robot vs. navigation" distinction is a marketing frame, not a clinical one.** The mechanism of action of ROSA (cutting guide) and OrthoPilot (cutting guide) is closer than Stryker would have the market believe. The head-to-head implant-alignment literature converges.
2. **The UK procurement centre of gravity is moving** from capital-led buys to total-cost-of-ownership and throughput-led buys (Clarivate 2025 commentary). That window favours B. Braun, **but it closes as Mako/ROSA installed base compounds and locks out implants**.

## 3. The problem statement

### 3.1 Primary statement

> The UK knee-arthroplasty market is being shaped by a narrative that equates "robot" with "best outcome" and "modern surgery." Hospitals are paying ~£1M of capital — plus service contracts and implant lock-in — to buy into that narrative, often without proportionate gain in clinical outcome or theatre efficiency. B. Braun has a navigation system (OrthoPilot Elite) that delivers clinically equivalent implant alignment at a fraction of the capex, with faster set-up and better list integrity, but is being commoditised in the conversation because it "isn't a robot." The gap is one of **framing and commercial storytelling, not of clinical substance.**

### 3.2 From B. Braun's point of view

- Technically validated product (>100 studies) being out-shouted by capital-robot marketing.
- Margin and the clinical story are weakened if B. Braun is positioned as "the navigation also-ran."
- Needs to move the buyer conversation off feature-parity (arm vs. guide) and onto **commercial outcomes** — throughput, list utilisation, capex avoidance, patient acquisition.
- No differentiated patient-acquisition / co-marketing programme today — this is an **open flank** (Stryker has set the bar with national DTC; B. Braun is not in the fight).

### 3.3 From the private-group / hospital decision-maker's point of view

- Under pressure to grow elective throughput (post-COVID backlog, RTT targets; private-sector margin & occupancy).
- Consultants lobbying for a robot because it helps them recruit patients and retain referrers.
- ~£1M capex (plus service, plus implant lock-in) is a very large bet against a list whose bottleneck is actually staffing, turnaround and admin friction.
- No clean way to test the claim "robots bring patients" — taken on faith.
- What is actually needed: **more patients through the door, predictable list times, clinical outcomes that don't embarrass the brand, and a technology story the marketing team can sell.**

### 3.4 From the consultant surgeon's point of view

- A robot is a recruitment tool — patients ask for it by name.
- But: loss of autonomy inside a locked ecosystem; capital decisions outrun their influence; case cadence slows if the robot adds set-up / turnover time.
- Wants the patient-pulling halo **without** the workflow tax and **without** being married to one implant family.

### 3.5 From the patient's point of view

- "Robotic = safer and more modern" — the story they have been advertised and the one they search for.
- Cannot assess alignment accuracy; chooses by brand cue, surgeon reputation and perceived modernity of the hospital.
- Price-insensitive (NHS-funded or insured); time- and recovery-sensitive.

## 4. Symptoms of the problem (for agreement before we solution)

1. **Capex bloat.** ~£1M list prices on robotic arms, plus service contracts and implant lock-in, that outlast the clinical case for them.
2. **Throughput tax.** Added set-up, CT planning, docking and turnover time per case compress the theatre list. **List integrity is the real commercial lever, and it is being eroded.**
3. **Clinical equivalence obscured.** Head-to-head data shows navigation-guided and robotic-arm cuts converge on implant alignment; patients and purchasers are sold a premium story not matched by the functional-outcomes evidence (JAAOS 2024 review — only "precision," "reduced tissue injury" and "less pain" hold up against the literature; other claims are contested).
4. **Implant lock-in.** Every "robot" ties a hospital to one manufacturer's implants and consumables; switching cost, not clinical case, becomes the stickiness.
5. **Patient-acquisition arms race.** Hospitals are buying robots as marketing assets as much as clinical ones. Stryker's national DTC engine has set the commercial bar; B. Braun has no equivalent.
6. **Clinician conflation.** Surgeons equate "robot" with recruitment pull — a real commercial effect, but not an exclusive property of capital robots. A navigation system with a proper patient-marketing wrap can replicate the pull.
7. **Procurement drift to value.** UK purchasing is moving from capital-led to total-cost-of-ownership and throughput-led criteria — a window for B. Braun, one that closes as the installed base of Mako/ROSA compounds.
8. **No commercial wrapper around OrthoPilot.** It is sold as a device, not as a commercial programme. Stryker sells a go-to-market engine; B. Braun currently sells a better jig.

## 5. Where this points (for the next conversation)

Reframe OrthoPilot Elite from _"navigation, not a robot"_ → **"the knee-arthroplasty commercial programme"**:

- **Clinically equivalent** alignment and outcomes, evidenced by >100 studies.
- **Lower capex** — protect the hospital's balance sheet.
- **Faster theatre turnaround** — protect list integrity and throughput (28% data-acquisition saving; image-free avoids CT scheduling lead-times).
- **No implant lock-in tax** — preserve surgeon autonomy and procurement optionality.
- **Wrapped in an award-grade patient-acquisition programme** — the feature competitors don't have and the one that makes the robot-vs-navigation argument commercially moot.

The sales motion changes with it: go to **private groups** with a throughput partnership (patient acquisition + list productivity) rather than a device pitch. Go to **NHS trusts** with a capex-avoidance story against the backdrop of tightened capital and RTT targets.
